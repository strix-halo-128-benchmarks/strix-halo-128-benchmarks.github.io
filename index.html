Executive summary

0.0 In plain terms

This is a test of 30 AI models on one small computer — a Mini-PC the size of a hardback book, with a single AMD chip and 128 GB of shared memory. Everything here runs on that machine, offline, with no cloud account. The question was simple: which of these models is worth using, and what does each one cost you in speed?

Five things came out of it.

  1. The best model on this machine is a small one. A 26-billion-parameter model in 15.6 GB is the best

all-round model I tested — first on knowledge among everything on disk, joint-first on code — and it is what answers my messages. The 35B and 120B files beside it beat it on neither axis.

  1. Bigger is not better, because speed comes from how much of a model runs per word. Most modern models are

mixtures: for each word they activate only a few of their many parts. A 120B model that wakes 5B parts per word can be faster than a dense 31B that wakes all of them — and on this machine that is the whole game, because the limit is how fast memory can feed the chip, not how much fits in it. The 120B-class models here are 2-2.5× slower than the 26B and score lower on both tests. They earn their 60 GB back for nothing measurable.

  1. Coding ability and knowledge are different skills, and a model can have one without the other. One 35B

model I tested writes good code (0.8598) and knows almost nothing (0.3250) — it answers a multiple-choice question with a guess in a fifth of a second. Another is the reverse. A single "quality" score hides this, so every model here is scored on both, plus a set of riddles that catches models which answer confidently and wrongly.

  1. Squeezing a model smaller costs knowledge before it costs skill. Compressed to 2 bits per weight, a model

still writes code at 0.9451 but drops to 0.3667 on knowledge — the facts go first. On the other end, an 8-bit version of a model was no better than its 4-bit sibling and ran 8 tokens/second slower, for 10.5 GB more disk. Four bits is the sweet spot here.

  1. Uncensoring is usually free — but only when it is done properly. Where a well-made uncensored build

existed, it scored within a couple of points of the original. Where a model looked badly damaged after uncensoring, the real cause turned out to be aggressive compression, not the uncensoring at all.

What it costs to run. A full test sweep draws about 0.14 kWh — roughly 3.5 pence. The machine idles at 6 watts, about £13 a year. The expensive part is the time, not the electricity.

What to use, in one line each. Chat and general work: the 26B driver on port 8100. Bulk classification or summarising: a 12B model at 111 tokens/second. A million-token document: Kimi-Linear. Images: MiniCPM-V. Every one of those is on disk and measured; anything that lost its slot has been deleted, and the ledger says why.

What this cannot tell you. Each score is 164 code problems and 120 knowledge questions, so a gap under about 5 points is noise, not a finding. One machine, one version of the software, one set of settings — another box, another build or another kernel will move speed by 10-20% while the quality scores hold. Everything below is written to make those limits visible rather than to hide them behind a single number.

0.1 The technical framing

Seven claims carry the rest of the document.

  1. Leaderboards do not answer the question. A published benchmark score tells you how a model behaves on

someone else's hardware, at someone else's prompt length, with someone else's sampler. On a bandwidth-limited integrated GPU, a model that is excellent in theory can be unusable in practice — too slow, too large, or quietly broken by a chat template. The only score that matters is the one this machine produces at the caps this machine will actually serve.

  1. The binding constraint is memory bandwidth, not capacity. With 128 GB unified and a 100 GiB

GPU-addressable pool, almost nothing this machine can run is too big. What separates models is how many bytes each generated token must read: a 26B model with 4B active parameters runs ~3x faster than a 31B dense model of similar size. Choosing a model here is mostly choosing an active-parameter count.

  1. A cheap gate catches most bad candidates. Three probes — non-empty reply, valid JSON, one-word answer —

cost under a second and rejected models that scored well on published code benchmarks. Run the gate first; do not spend an hour on HumanEval to discover the model repeats itself.

  1. Cap-burn is not the same as being wrong. These models are scored at a fixed generation cap. A reasoning

model that thinks past that cap returns nothing and is marked failed. Reading a score without reading the cap and the burn count is how good models get discarded and bad ones kept.

  1. Quantisation on this machine is a performance decision before it is a capacity decision. Decode is

bandwidth-bound, so fewer bits per weight means more tokens per second — measured at +22% and +43% in two pairs. The conventional "quantise only to fit" framing is a CUDA habit and gives the wrong answer here.

  1. Results do not transfer across a family in the way intuition suggests. Code scores travel within

±2-3pp across a family at fixed quantisation; knowledge moved by 16.7pp and 50pp inside two families here, and speed does not transfer at all. §10 sets out exactly what may be inferred.

  1. The frontier's lead is axis-specific, and smaller than expected. Measured with this harness, the best

hosted model is 1.2pp ahead of the best local model on code — inside the noise — but 5.8pp ahead on knowledge, and the cheap hosted tier scores below a local 30B on knowledge. §14 has the numbers, the cache economics and the confounds.

0.2 Conventions used throughout

MMLU in a compact score or table cell means MMLU-Pro (the 120-item chat 0-shot probe described in §6.2), never the original 14,000-item MMLU; the full name is written out wherever the distinction matters. GTT is the Graphics Translation Table — the GPU-addressable window into system RAM, which on this machine is the 100 GiB pool that actually matters (§2.1), not the 8 GiB reported as VRAM. SE is the standard error of a proportion, quoted whenever two scores are close enough that sampling noise could explain the gap; at n=164 that is ±2.3 pp at p=0.9, at n=120 it is ±2.7 pp (§8 has the full table).

0.3 What exists as a result

A roster of 15 servable models with measured profiles; a deployed daily driver — the uncensored QAT Q4_K_M build of gemma4-26b-a4b on port 8100, which since 2026-10-28 is what answers WhatsApp, with Ornith-1.5-35B-Uncensored on :8094 as the system boot model; a documented set of models that were measured, failed or lost their slot and were deleted, with hashes and restore recipes; and a measured comparison against hosted frontier models that establishes how large the gap actually is. Port 8099 (the QAT Q4_0 driver named in earlier editions) still has its script but its unit is disabled; only one model unit is enabled at a time and :8100 holds that slot.

Why test at all — the problem this solves

The obvious approach to picking a local model is to read a leaderboard, pick the top entry that fits in memory, and run it. That approach fails on this class of hardware for four independent reasons.

Reason 1: the hardware changes the ranking. Benchmarks are usually run on data-centre GPUs with tensor cores and hundreds of GB/s of HBM bandwidth. This machine has an integrated GPU with no tensor cores and roughly a quarter of that bandwidth. Models are not uniformly penalised: a dense 30B model and a 26B mixture-of-experts model of the same file size can differ by 4x in tokens per second. Published scores cannot tell you which side of that cliff a model sits on.

Reason 2: quantisation changes capability, not just size. A model at Q4 is not the model at BF16. Most published scores are BF16. Here, some models hold up well at 4 bits and some collapse — one 74B model scores 0.9451 on code at 2.06 bits per weight while another 74B model of the same architecture family scores 0.1786. Same nominal recipe, wildly different outcome. Only measurement distinguishes them.

Reason 3: served configuration is part of the result. A model's behaviour depends on its chat template, whether reasoning is enabled, the KV-cache quantisation, the context length it was loaded with, and the sampling parameters the server applies. One model here failed 20 of 20 code items purely because its thinking mode was on and consumed the entire generation budget before emitting an answer; with thinking off it scored 0.8659. Nothing about the weights changed. Any evaluation that does not pin the serving configuration is not reproducible.

Reason 4: the failure mode that matters is total, not incremental. For an agent workload, a model that emits invalid JSON once in twenty replies is not "95% good" — it breaks the loop. Three probes catch this class of failure in under a second. That is why the harness has a gate before the long tests rather than after.

The goal is therefore not "which model is best" in the abstract. It is: for each axis of capability this machine will be asked to perform, which model is the best available that is actually servable here, and what does it cost in speed and memory to use it.

The platform: what Strix Halo actually is

The box is a Minisforum MS-S1 MAX — product_family: MINISFORUM, product_name: MS-S1 MAX, sys_vendor: Micro Computer (HK) Tech Limited, board SHWSA (Meigao ODM), BIOS 1.06, EC firmware 0.15. All of that is readable from DMI on the machine itself, which is the point of quoting it: it is checkable rather than asserted. The hostname is deliberately not published — it is the operator's, not the hardware's.

Two numbers describe this machine and explain most of its behaviour: about 100 GiB of GPU-addressable memory, and roughly 256 GB/s of bandwidth shared between CPU and GPU.

Two numbers explain everything about this machine's behaviour:

  • GTT = 100 GiB. This is the address space the GPU can map. It is not separate memory; it is the

same LPDDR5X, mapped for the GPU. A 59 GB model fits. A 42.9 GiB model fits with room for KV cache. There is no 24 GB wall to design around, which is the single biggest difference from a consumer Nvidia card. - ~256 GB/s of bandwidth, shared between CPU and GPU. Every generated token requires reading the active weights once. Bandwidth therefore sets the ceiling on tokens per second, and it is a shared resource: if the CPU is busy, decode slows.

2.2 Why this makes active parameters the key number

The dense model is smarter on knowledge (MMLU 0.8667 vs 0.8417) and 3x slower. That trade-off, not raw parameter count, is the central decision on this box: you are almost always choosing between "reads 16 GB per token" and "reads 3 GB per token".

Measured effective bandwidth across the roster lands between roughly 120 and 280 GB/s depending on quantisation, expert routing and context length. That spread is why the axis tables are empirical and why a formula will only ever predict a new model to within ~40%. Use the tables; predict with the formula only to rule a model out.

2.3 Storage and paging — what the drives do and do not affect

Worth one paragraph because it is the component most often over-attributed in local-LLM write-ups.

   system + models   BIWIN X570 PRO 4 TB NVMe      ~600 GiB of GGUF live here; the root and /home
                                                   filesystems share one btrfs partition
   spare             Netac 2 TB NVMe               unmounted, no filesystem in use
   swap              /dev/zram0, 117 GiB           compressed RAM (priority 100), vm.swappiness 150

Storage affects load time and nothing else measured in this report. Decode is bound by LPDDR5X bandwidth (~256 GB/s shared with the CPU) and prefill by shader occupancy; neither reads the SSD once the weights are resident. A model loads from NVMe at the drive's sequential rate — a 42.9 GiB file appears in a few tens of seconds — and is then faulted into GTT and never re-read.

Paging is deliberately not disk-backed. Swap is a zram device, so any pressure costs CPU to compress and decompress rather than NVMe reads. That distinction matters when reading a benchmark: a model whose weights were paged out still pays a fault-in on its next request, but it pays it in cycles, not in I/O. Every run recorded here was taken with the server's weights resident, and §5 explains how the pool is sized to keep them that way.

3.2 Why Vulkan rather than ROCm or CUDA

   └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

Every number in this document was produced on the Vulkan backend. That is a deliberate choice:

  • ROCm/HIP for gfx1151 is workable but not the mature path. Support has improved through 2026,

but the Vulkan backend needs no ROCm install, no version pinning, and no per-model kernel selection. - CUDA does not exist here and neither does the CUDA software ecosystem: vLLM, TensorRT-LLM, flash-attention kernels, bitsandbytes — all unavailable. This is the single largest practical difference from an Nvidia box, and it is why the harness talks to llama-server's OpenAI-compatible endpoint rather than to a serving framework. - Vulkan has one visible cost: the first run of a new model or a new quantisation pays shader compilation. Benches warm up before measuring for exactly this reason; without the warmup, the first numbers are 2-5x too low.

The runtime: three llama.cpp trees and why

Three trees exist because architecture support is not uniform across llama.cpp builds, and what each one is permitted to load is a deliberate boundary rather than an accident of history.

Figure 4 — The three llama.cpp trees and what each is permitted to do.

   ~/llama-local/
   ├── halo-box-strix-llama.cpp   ◄── SERVES EVERY UNIT
   │     Vulkan, GGML_NATIVE, custom kernels + MTP/dflash support.
   │     Build 0.5.0-dev, commit 1a33095. This is the production binary.
   │
   ├── prism-llama.cpp            ◄── exotic quant loader
   │     PrismML `prism` branch. Required for type-142 (PQ2_0) and
   │     blockwise-Hadamard weights that stock trees reject outright.
   │
   ├── upstream-llama.cpp         ◄── reference / new-arch bench tree
   ├── krafton-llama.cpp          ◄── LLM_ARCH_RAON1 only exists here
   └── sensenova-llama.cpp        ◄── has LLM_ARCH_SENSENOVA_U1, no converter

Three trees exist because architecture support is not uniform. ggml-org/llama.cpp is the reference, but a model can be released against a vendor fork months before upstream support lands; and a quantisation can require a loader patch that upstream does not carry. Keeping the trees separate means a new architecture can be tested without endangering the binary that serves the WhatsApp channel.

Build flags that matter on this box (from build/CMakeCache.txt):

-DCMAKE_BUILD_TYPE=Release
-DGGML_VULKAN=ON                     # the only GPU backend used
-DGGML_NATIVE=ON                     # tune for this Zen 5 / RDNA 3.5 part
-DGGML_CCACHE=OFF                    # ccache caused stale-kernel confusion
-DLLAMA_CURL=OFF                     # no runtime downloads in server
-DVulkan_INCLUDE_DIR=~/.local/include
-DVulkan_LIBRARY=/usr/lib/libvulkan.so

The last two are not optional: CMake's Vulkan discovery fails (FPHSA "Could NOT find Vulkan") on this distro without them, which is the most common build failure reported for Strix Halo.

Four things the register settles, and one it refuses to:

  • The "0.5.0-dev" version belongs to the fork, not to the project. Rows measured on the same day

on upstream-master-bolt ran 0.7.0-dev, two minor versions ahead, because the trees are independent checkouts rather than branches of one repository. A score is not portable across flavours, and the ggml ABI moves with the flavour: 0.21 to 0.27 across this roster.

  • 2026-10-16 is a discontinuity inside a single flavour. 38 fork rows were measured on

4b2561e48 and 24 on 1a3309516 — the Bolt/Nemotron-VL cherry-pick and its follow-up revert. Rows either side of that rebuild are not the same binary, whatever the path says.

  • Most of the archive predates identity stamping, so its commits are inferred rather than

recorded: exact where one commit was HEAD across the whole window, window where a rebuild could have carried either. One row (gemma4-26b-a4b, 10-15 22:08) sits in that window and is a caveat on that measurement, not a fact about it.

  • flint's 10-21 → 10-25 rows cannot be attributed at all. That tree was re-cloned on 10-27 and

its earlier HEAD is gone, so those runs are labelled by tree and date only. This is precisely the cost of not recording the commit at the time.

  • What the register does not claim: that a commit is a binary. A tree can be rebuilt from the

same commit with different CMake flags, and only build_dirty plus the flags in §4 distinguish that case. Commit identity is necessary provenance, not sufficient.

From here on, every run's identity row carries build_tree, build_abi, build_commit, build_dirty, build_info (the server's own string from /props, e.g. b3-1a33095) and build_version (from llama-server --version), all written by benchlib.build_identity() before the first test item runs. Legacy rows were annotated once with ~/bench/annotate-builds.py.

Memory accounting and quantisation

Weights are only part of the footprint. KV cache scales linearly with context length and is charged to the same 100 GiB pool:

   TOTAL GPU FOOTPRINT  =  weights  +  KV cache  +  compute buffers  +  mmproj (vision)

   weights        fixed by the quantisation        e.g.  13.4 GiB  (26B A4B at Q4_0)
   KV cache       ctx_tokens x layers x kv_heads x head_dim x 2 x bytes_per_elem
                  at q8_0 that halves; at f16 it doubles the q8_0 figure
   compute        ~1-2 GiB, grows with batch and ubatch
   mmproj         vision projector, 0.8-1.6 GiB when the model is multimodal

   WORKED EXAMPLE — a 4B model loaded at a genuine 1,048,576-token context:
     weights 3.3 GiB  +  KV 20.0 GiB  +  buffers ~1 GiB  =  ~24 GiB for a 4B model
     observed GTT: 35.5 -> 55.5 GiB once loaded

Two practical consequences:

  1. Context length is a memory purchase, and it is often the expensive part. Serving a small

model at a million tokens can cost more memory than serving a large model at 32k. The roster's default context is 32k-131k, not "maximum", for this reason. 2. Quantise the KV cache. --cache-type-k q8_0 --cache-type-v q8_0 roughly halves KV footprint with no measurable quality cost on the axes tested here, and is applied to every unit.

How the pool is enforced — one resident model at a time, by mutual exclusion — is the subject of §6. The remaining question about bytes is the encoding itself: how many bits each weight is stored in, and what that costs in quality, capacity and speed. On this machine that is not a packaging detail but a first-order performance decision, for the reason set out next.

5.1 What quantisation buys on this machine — speed, not just size

The standard framing is that quantisation trades quality for memory. On a discrete GPU that is accurate: you quantise to make a model fit, and the card's bandwidth was never the limit. On Strix Halo the trade is different, and it is worth stating plainly because it changes how the choice should be made.

Decode is bandwidth-bound: each generated token requires reading the active weights once. Fewer bytes per weight therefore means more tokens per second, near-linearly, until some other limit bites. On this box, quantising is a performance decision first and a capacity decision second.

Figure 5 — Bytes read per token determine decode speed.

   same 12.1B model, two quantisations            same 30.1B model, two quantisations

   Q6_K   10.1 GiB  ──►  91.8 t/s                Q8_0   34.6 GiB  ──►  52.3 t/s
   Q4_K_M  7.5 GiB  ──► 111.9 t/s (+22%)         Q4_K_M 17.0 GiB  ──►  74.7 t/s (+43%)
            ▲                                              ▲
            └── also 2.6 GiB smaller                       └── also half the memory

The consequence is a genuinely different optimum from the CUDA world. There, Q4 is the common default because it fits; here it is the default because it is faster as well as smaller, and the only reason to climb the ladder is a measurable quality gain that you can justify on an axis you care about. The next subsection quantifies what those gains actually are on this hardware.

Three conclusions that are not the conventional ones:

  1. Quantisation does not uniformly cost quality. In two of four pairs the smaller or more

aggressively quantised variant scored better on at least one axis — the importance-matrix Q4_K_M beats the vendor Q6_K by 3.7pp code and 10.8pp knowledge, and the 8-bit→4-bit QAT step in the gemma pair costs only 0.6pp of code. Quantisation error is not monotonic in bits per weight when the recipe changes as well. 2. Knowledge is the axis that moves; code barely does. Across the four pairs code moved by at most 3.7pp and usually by under 1.2pp, while knowledge moved from −2.5pp to +16.7pp. If you must quantise aggressively and you care about facts, you are spending the wrong currency. 3. A good 4-bit is the right default, and QAT is worth preferring when it exists. The gemma QAT file gives up 0.6pp of code and 2.5pp of knowledge for 21% more decode and 11.6 GiB less resident memory — the difference between a 25 GiB and a 13.4 GiB footprint, which on a box that also runs CCTV recording and a hardware monitor is not a rounding error. What the channel actually runs is the uncensored Q4_K_M (0.9573 / 0.8500 at 56.9 t/s), chosen for refusal behaviour first: at the 4-bit rung the three gemma encodings are 0.6-0.8pp apart on code and 0.8-2.5pp on knowledge, so behaviour, speed and footprint — not the last code point — decide it.

5.4 Naming: K-quants, I-quants, UD and QAT are not interchangeable

Four labels get treated as synonyms and are not. The distinction matters because it explains why a "Q4" is sometimes better than a "Q6".

   K-quant   Q4_K_M, Q5_K_M, Q6_K     block-wise, the mature default; M = medium mix
   I-quant   IQ2_XXS, IQ3_S, IQ4_XS   importance-matrix driven; smaller at equal quality,
                                      but more sensitive to a bad imatrix — and the source
                                      of every catastrophic row in this document
   UD        UD-Q4_K_XL, UD-Q6_K_XL   "unsloth dynamic": per-tensor quantisation, sensitive
                                      tensors kept at higher precision. Not comparable to
                                      plain Q4_K_M at the same nominal label.
   QAT       gemma-4-26B_q4_0-it      quantisation-aware training: the model was trained to
                                      tolerate the quantisation it ships with

Practical consequence: never compare across naming schemes by the number alone. UD-Q4_K_XL is larger and better than Q4_K_M; IQ4_XS is smaller than Q4_K_M and roughly comparable in quality when the imatrix is good and much worse when it is not. The only reliable comparison is the one made on this hardware, which is why the roster records the exact file name and bpw next to every score.

5.5 KV-cache quantisation is a separate decision

Weight quantisation and KV-cache quantisation are independent, and conflating them leads to wrong predictions about context cost. Measured on this box by loading the driver at two context lengths and differencing the GTT footprint:

   gemma4-26b-qat-q4, q8_0 KV, single slot
     @ 16,384 tokens   GTT +14.46 GiB
     @131,072 tokens   GTT +15.84 GiB
     ──────────────────────────────────────
     Δ 1.38 GiB over 114,688 tokens  →  12.6 KB per token
     implied KV:  0.39 GiB @32k  ·  1.58 GiB @131k  ·  ~3.2 GiB @131k if KV were f16

Two observations worth carrying away:

  • Context is cheap for this architecture and expensive for others. 12.6 KB/token is small

because most of the model's attention layers are sliding-window, so only a minority of layers grow with context. Contrast the 4B Spark-X2.6 measured the same way: ~20 KB/token, 20 GiB for a 1M-token window — six times more KV per token from a model a sixth the size. - The saving from q8_0 KV is real but bounded. Halving KV precision halves the KV term; if the KV term is 1.6 GiB at 131k, the saving is 1.6 GiB, not 13 GiB. Decide based on measurement for the specific architecture, not on a rule of thumb.

Every unit on this box runs --cache-type-k q8_0 --cache-type-v q8_0. On the axes measured here it cost nothing detectable, and it is the reason 131k contexts fit alongside a 74 GiB model in the same session budget.

6.3 Bench isolation

Benchmarks run through transient units (systemd-run --user --collect --unit=bench-<name>), which do not participate in Conflicts=. The harness

Seven axes, each answering exactly one question, scored by exact match or executed code rather than by another model — and one knob, the generation cap, that moves scores further than most model changes do.

7.1 Design principles

~/bench is a small, self-contained harness with six rules that turned out to matter more than any individual test:

  1. No external judge, no API calls. Every axis is scored by exact match, executable test, or

JSON parse. Nothing depends on a model grading a model, so results are reproducible offline and a network outage cannot silently change a score. 2. Caps are recorded per row. The generation cap, the item count, and the quantisation go into ~/runs/catalogue.json next to the score. A number without its cap is not a result. 3. The gate runs before the expensive axes. Preflight is cheap and diagnostic; running it last wastes the hour that HumanEval costs. 4. Report empty answers separately from wrong answers. These are different failures — one is a budget artefact, the other is a capability limit — and conflating them is how models get misjudged. 5. Every axis writes to JSONL as it completes. A crashed or aborted run still yields partial evidence, which is the difference between "unknown" and "abandoned for a documented reason". 6. Record the build, not just the tree. The identity row carries the serving commit, the ggml ABI, the server's own build string and a dirty flag (§4.1). Without them, a re-measure after a git pull looks identical to the original run — which is the gap that had to be reconstructed by hand for everything measured before 2026-10-27.

7.2 The seven axes

   ┌──────────────┬──────────────────────────────────────┬──────────┬────────────────────┐
   │ AXIS         │ WHAT IT MEASURES                     │ COST     │ SCORED BY          │
   ├──────────────┼──────────────────────────────────────┼──────────┼────────────────────┤
   │ perf         │ prefill, decode, time-to-first-token │ seconds  │ llama timings      │
   │ preflight    │ gate: does it reply, JSON, one word   │ <1 s     │ exact / JSON parse │
   │ needle 2k/8k │ retrieval from mid-context           │ seconds  │ exact substring    │
   │ riddles      │ 14 reasoning + arithmetic traps      │ minutes  │ exact match        │
   │ HumanEval    │ 164 coding problems, unit-tested     │ 20 min-2h│ pytest execution  │
   │ MMLU-Pro     │ 120 knowledge MCQs                   │ 20-50 min│ exact letter       │
   │ IFEval       │ 30 instruction-following constraints │ 10 min   │ lm-eval strict acc │
   └──────────────┴──────────────────────────────────────┴──────────┴────────────────────┘

perf — warms up, then sends a ~2000-token prompt and asks for a fixed 128-token reply with prompt caching disabled. Reports prefill tokens/s (pp), decode tokens/s (tg) and time-to-first- token. The warmup exists because Vulkan shader compilation and cold page cache distort the first measurement by 2-5x.

preflight — three probes, each chosen because it catches a specific real failure:

   content_nonempty      "Reply with exactly: OK"
       catches: broken chat template, immediate EOS, empty output

   json_schema           'Return a JSON object with exactly one key "status" and value "ok"'
       catches: tool-calling breakage. An agent that cannot emit clean JSON
                cannot call a tool. Observed failure: repeating the same object
                four times back to back.

   instruction_one_word  "What is the capital of France? Answer with exactly one word."
       catches: models that argue with the format. Observed failure: "Paris. Wait,
                let me reconsider—the period might count as punctuation…"

A model that fails any of these is not served, regardless of its code score. One measured model (REAP50-pruned Ornith) scored well on its publisher's HumanEval harness and failed 2 of these 3.

needle 2k / 8k — a unique token is planted in the middle of filler text and must be quoted back. Deliberately generous on budget (256 tokens, retried at 4x if the model ran out while thinking) so that "cannot retrieve" is not confused with "ran out of room to think". This is a floor test: failing it disqualifies a long-context model, passing it does not prove long-context quality.

riddles + arithmetic (14 probes) — five classic riddles and arithmetic traps. The traps are the valuable part: 9.11 vs 9.9 catches lexicographic float comparison, and the bat-and-ball problem catches the intuitive-but-wrong 10-cent answer. Tested with exact-match on a substring list, so a correct answer inside a verbose explanation still passes.

HumanEval (164 problems) — the code axis, and the only axis where scoring is objective by construction: candidate function bodies are executed against unit tests. A model cannot bluff a passing implementation. This is the most expensive axis (20 minutes to 2 hours) and the one most worth running only after preflight.

MMLU-Pro (120 questions) — four-option knowledge questions, scored on the exact option letter. Separates "knows things" from "can code", which are frequently different models here: the fastest model on this machine (mellum21, 111.9 t/s) is mid-pack on knowledge, and the best knowledge model on record (gemma4-31b, retired 2026-10-30) was the second slowest; the best one still on disk (gemma4-26b-uncen, 0.8500) runs at 56.9 t/s.

IFEval (30 prompts) — instruction-following constraints (lowercase only, exactly N paragraphs, JSON with specific keys). Run through lm-eval's local-chat-completions task. This axis carries the harness's biggest known trap, described next.

How to read the results that follow

Five rules, in order of how much damage ignoring them causes:

  1. A score without a cap is meaningless. Every row below states its cap where it differs from

the axis default (HumanEval/MMLU 2048, riddles 4096). 2. n matters. qwen35 at 0.9242 is a 66-item measurement; gemma4-31b at 0.9634 is 164 items. Preflight 0/3 does not mean the model is bad at its job. minicpm-v47-35b fails all three preflight probes and is still this machine's best vision model. 9.1 Per-model analysis

gemma4-26b-uncen — the deployed driver, and the current ceiling. 0.9573 code, 0.8500 MMLU, 14/14 riddles, 3/3 preflight, both needles, 56.9 t/s decode at 1759 t/s prefill, 15.6 GiB resident, 131k context. Measured 2026-10-28 and serving since: llama-gemma4-26b-uncen.service on :8100 is the only enabled model unit and it is what answers WhatsApp. It is the best HumanEval and the best MMLU-Pro on disk, and the uncensoring costs nothing measurable — same architecture, same quant rung, same speed class as the QAT file below.

gemma4-26b-qat-q4 — the non-uncensored alternative. One weight file, two runs: 0.9512 (156/164) in the 2026-10-21 matched run and 0.9390 (154/164) in the 2026-10-17 run, MMLU 0.8417 (101/120) in both, 59.5 t/s, 13.4 GiB resident, 131k context. Version 1.2 listed those two runs as two different models; they are the same file. It keeps the :8099 script as the fallback if the uncensored weights are ever distrusted, but its unit is disabled because :8100 already holds the channel slot.

gemma4-26b-a4b Q8_0 — retired 2026-10-30, and its row was genuine. It was re-measured on 2026-10-30 specifically to test a suspicion that its stored numbers had been borrowed from the driver. They had not: 0.9573 (157/164) and 0.8667 (104/120) at cap 2048, reproducing the 2026-10-17 row item for item on a different kernel. It went on size and speed, not on distrust of the number — the Q4_K_M posts 0.9573 and 0.8500 for 10.5 GiB less and 8 t/s more, and the difference is 2 items (1.7 pp) against a 3.3 pp standard error. Its numbers stay in Table 4 because 0.8667 is the highest knowledge score ever measured on this box.

minicpm-v47-35b — the vision specialist. 0.9573 code at a 4096 cap (second best on code, and measured above the a4b), 0.7750 MMLU, 64.6 t/s, and it is multimodal with a 1.0 GiB projector. It fails preflight 0/3 for a benign reason: its template expects image content and refuses bare chat probes. Treat the 0/3 as a template artefact, not a quality signal — but do not wire it behind a text-only agent interface either.

ornith-122b — the best big model. 124.6B at Q4_K_M (74 GiB), 0.9512 code, 0.7917 MMLU, 23.3 t/s and 605 t/s prefill. This is the honest big-model result on Strix Halo: 74 GiB fits, it works, it is slightly better on code than the 26B driver and clearly worse on speed. Its niche is difficult code and long single-shot reasoning where 20 seconds of latency is acceptable.

nemotron-super-120b — biggest and least ergonomic. 123.6B at Q3_K_M, 0.9512 code, 0.7667 MMLU, 27.1 t/s but only 411 t/s prefill and the weakest instruction-following of the large models (0.600/0.533). It is beaten by the 26B driver on knowledge and instruction, and by the 122B on prefill. Kept because it is genuinely 120B-class and sometimes size alone matters, not because it wins an axis.

Nemotron-Bolt-74B-A9B at IQ2_XXS — retired 2026-10-30, kept as the quantisation warning. 0.9451 code (excellent), 13/14 riddles, 72.8 t/s — and 0.3667 MMLU, 44 of 120 items at 2.06 bits per weight. The model retained procedural skill and lost factual knowledge almost entirely, and no cap-4096 arm exists for this file, so the knowledge axis could not be recovered by budget. Its sibling load at the same nominal quant (nemotron-bolt row) scored 0.1786 on code, i.e. it broke. Two lessons survive the deletion: 2-bit quants can preserve one axis while destroying another, and two files with the same recipe can behave completely differently.

qwen3next-q6 — the reasoning generalist. 79.7B Q6_K, 0.9207 code, 14/14 riddles, 45.8 t/s. Weak knowledge (0.6058) but the best instruction-following of any large model here (0.885/0.923) and perfect on the trap probes. The long-standing personal favourite for exactly this reason: it follows instructions precisely at a usable speed. Retired from the driver seat only because the gemma QAT is faster and stronger on knowledge.

qwen3next-q4 — same family, three times the footprint, no gain. Measured 2026-10-31, the first capability row this file has ever had: 0.9146 code (150/164) and 0.7417 knowledge (89/120) at cap 2048, 14/14 riddles, both needles, 3/3 preflight, 58.1 t/s decode at 1219 t/s prefill. Against the driver that is −4.3 pp code and −10.8 pp knowledge for 42.9 GiB instead of 15.6 — at the same decode speed. It also loses to gpt-oss-abliterated, an 18.7 GiB file, on both axes. The 80B/3B-active architecture spreads its active experts too thin: it matches the Q6 sibling on code (0.9146 vs 0.9296 over 142 items, inside noise) but the knowledge axis shows what the extra 42 GiB of weights buys, which is nothing. Retire candidate on evidence, not on opinion.

mellum21-fork — the speed champion and best value. 12.1B with 2.5B active, 111.9 t/s decode, 2723 t/s prefill, 0.9146 code at an 8192 cap, 0.8083 MMLU. Nothing else on this box comes close on throughput per byte, and 0.91 code from a 7.5 GiB file is remarkable. The catch is the 8192 cap: at 4096 it truncates mid-derivation and scores lower. It is also a fork-specific build. Use it for high-volume, low-latency work — classification, extraction, triage — not for hard reasoning.

lfm25-24b-a2b — the fastest 24B. 106.6 t/s, 2930 t/s prefill, 24B with 2B active. Lower quality (0.8537 code, 0.6917 MMLU) but the most responsive model of its size class, with a 1M-token context option. Its IFEval 0.3333 in the archive is an lm-eval artefact; standalone at a 4096 cap it measures 0.9333, which is one of the best instruction scores on this machine.

Kimi-Linear-48B-A3B-abl — the long-context file, and the honest version of a retired one. The abliterated i1-Q4_K_M build (27.66 GiB, 49.1B with ~3B active) measures 0.7866 code, 0.5833 knowledge, 13-14/14 riddles at 74.8 t/s — the same code score, item for item, as the safety-tuned kimi retired on 2026-10-29 (0.7866 / 0.5500 at 67.4 t/s), so the uncensoring cost nothing measurable and this file is 10% faster. What it buys is native context: 1,048,576 tokens, the only million-token window on this roster, and that is the reason to keep 28 GiB of a model that two smaller ones beat on both scored axes.

One constraint travels with it: n_embd_head_v = 72 does not divide a q8_0 cache block of 32, and the model refuses mismatched K/V cache types, so the KV cache must be f16 on both sides. At ctx 131072 that does not fit beside the 15.6 GiB driver; ctx 65536 does (GTT 59.5 of 100 GiB) and is what the published row was measured at. The full million-token window therefore needs the driver stopped.

hy-memory-30b-a3b — retired 2026-10-29. 74.7 t/s, 0.7805 code, 0.7250 MMLU, and a Q8 variant that raised knowledge to 0.7500 while dropping to 52.3 t/s. Retired as a redundant 30B: 17 GiB for a knowledge row 12.5 pp behind the 26B driver at two-thirds the speed. The Q4/Q8 pair remains the cleanest quant-ladder datapoint in the archive (§10.3).

qwen38-35b / qwen36-35b / ornith35b / Ornith-1.5-35B — the 35B trough. All four land between 0.82 and 0.87 code with mediocre knowledge and 57-75 t/s. They are not bad; they are simply dominated by the 26B MoE (faster and better) and the 122B (slower but much better).

Two of the four were re-measured at cap 4096 on 2026-11-01, and the two verdicts differ on which axis the cap was suppressing. qwen38-35b gained 3 HumanEval items (0.8537 → 0.8720) but 16 MMLU items (0.5083 → 0.6417, +13.3 pp, ≫ SE at n=120) and its riddles went 13/14 → 14/14: for that model the 512 cap was cutting off reasoning before the answer, and the damage showed up in knowledge far more than in code. Ornith-1.5-35B (Q4_K_M) gained 2 HumanEval items (0.8232 → 0.8354) and 3 knowledge items (0.4167 → 0.4417, +2.5 pp, inside SE) — a row that was close to honest already, and whose remaining weakness is the model, not the budget. So the lesson is not "512 understates" or "512 is fine": it is that the cap's damage is axis-dependent, and a single axis read at 512 cannot tell you which case you are in.

ornith35b is the exception, and it was re-measured on 2026-10-31 to prove it: at cap 4096 it scores 0.5671 code (93/164) and 0.6333 knowledge (76/120), against 0.5610 / 0.6000 at cap 512. One HumanEval item and four MMLU items — inside noise at those sample sizes. So the 512-token cap was not truncating this model: an aggressive IQ3_XXS load on an IQ3-class file really is a ~0.57 coder, and its riddles fell 13/14 → 12/14 when one arithmetic item (9.9 → 9.11) went wrong. The useful conclusion is the reverse of the qwen38-35b case: a 512 cap sometimes hides capability, and sometimes it tells you the truth, and only re-measuring distinguishes them.

gptoss / gptoss-abliterated — the 120B MoE pair. Both 116.8B MXFP4 at 59 GiB, both 0.9268 code at ~50 t/s, riddles 12/14 and 10/14. Their knowledge rows are not comparable: the base was measured at a 512-token cap and the abliterated build at 2048 (§16.2 shows the pair in full), so the apparent 0.17 gap is a token budget, not the weights. Useful when you want 120B-class breadth at 50 t/s and can afford 59 GiB.

qwen38fn-abl — the knowledge collapse. 176.9B at IQ3_S: 0.8476 code, 0.0083 MMLU (1 of 120). Something is badly wrong with this quantisation — the model can write code but cannot answer a multiple-choice question. Best example in the archive of why preflight-plus-MMLU belongs next to HumanEval: a code score alone would have made this look like a 177B keeper.

graft-50b-a3b — perfect riddles, ordinary code. 14/14 when almost nothing else scores 14/14, but 0.7805 code and 0.6583 knowledge. A curiosity rather than a pick.

Clover-35B-A3B-Preview — diffusion pretraining, AR-mode prices. Its riddles print 14/14, but three of those returned exactly 4096 tokens on the zebra puzzles and matched their own restatement, so the committed rate is 11/14. The one thing that would have made it interesting — parallel, self-speculative diffusion decode — is an unmerged upstream PR, so what was measured is the AR fallback at AR prices. Groot-65B-A6B — a 116.5B model that is honest about its size. Can you extrapolate from one model to another in the same family?

This is the question that decides how much of this document you can reuse. If results transferred across a family, one careful measurement of a 26B model would tell you what to expect from its 31B sibling, from its abliterated variant, and from its Q4 requantisation. They do not transfer uniformly, and the pattern of where they fail is consistent enough to state as rules.

10.1 Four things "same family" can mean

The phrase is used for four different relationships, which is the root of most bad extrapolation:

   (a) SAME WEIGHTS, DIFFERENT ENCODING     gemma4-26b-qat-q4  vs  gemma4-26b-a4b
       only quantisation differs. The most predictable relationship on this box.

   (b) SAME BASE, DIFFERENT FINETUNE        gptoss  vs  gptoss-abliterated
       abliteration, distillation, uncensoring. Per-axis shifts, direction not
       predictable without measurement.

   (c) SAME FAMILY, DIFFERENT SIZE          gemma4-26b-*  vs  gemma4-31b
       ordering is usually preserved on knowledge and code; absolute numbers and
       speed are not.

   (d) SAME LINEAGE NAME, DIFFERENT ARCH    qwen35  vs  qwen36  vs  qwen38
       NOT a family for extrapolation. Different architectures that share a
       branding convention. Treat as unrelated models.

Any statement of the form "the X family scores about Y" silently assumes one of these and usually means (c) while quoting evidence from (a). Distinguishing them is most of the work.

10.3 What the spreads establish

Code is the one axis that survives family extrapolation — but only at comparable quantisation. The two dense families that were not damaged by quantisation show a spread of 1.8-2.4pp: qwen3.6/3.8 across seven models from 0.8354 to 0.8537, gemma4 across three from 0.9390 to 0.9634. If you know one member of such a family scores 0.94 on HumanEval, a sibling at the same quant is very likely within ±3pp. That is a usable prediction.

Knowledge is the axis where the confounds live, which is not the same as being fragile. The two double-digit spreads in Table 5 — gpt-oss at 16.7pp and qwen3.6/3.8 at 50pp — are a token-budget difference and a 3.4-bit quantisation respectively, not lineage (§16.2 shows the gpt-oss pair in full). Where caps and quants are matched the picture is calm: gemma4's three models sit 2.5pp apart on knowledge and hy-memory's Q4/Q8 pair 2.5pp apart, both at identical caps. So the honest rule is narrower than "knowledge never transfers": knowledge transfers between variants measured the same way, and it is the axis most easily corrupted by a cap change, a quant rung or a finetune. Check those three before attributing a knowledge gap to the family at all.

The 0.39 and 0.77 spreads are not family variance at all. They are quantisation and load failures inside the group: ornith35b at IQ3_XXS (0.5671, cap 4096) against ornith-122b at Q4_K_M (0.9512), and nemotron-bolt (0.1786, a broken load) against Nemotron-Bolt-74B at IQ2_XXS (0.9451). This is the central trap. A "same family" comparison that spans quantisation levels measures the encoder, not the lineage, and it will happily produce a 0.77 spread that has nothing to do with the models' design.

Speed never transfers. gemma4 spans 17-59 t/s and hy-memory spans 52-75 t/s within families. Decode rate is set by active parameters, quantisation and build flags — 'family' predicts none of them. If you need a throughput estimate, measure it or compute it from bytes-per-token; do not infer it from a sibling.

Instruction adherence and preflight do not transfer either. These are properties of the chat template and the post-training recipe, both of which change between variants. One measured case: the REAP50 pruning of Ornith-122B kept a respectable code score and failed two of three preflight probes while its base passed all three. A model that is a good agent is not evidence that its siblings are.

10.5 Threats to validity

An honest account of what these results cannot support, because the same caveats apply to anyone reading them as a benchmark:

  • n = 1 run per model per axis. No seed variance, no repeated runs, no confidence intervals. The

HumanEval means carry ~1.3pp standard error at 164 items and ~3.5pp at 60; MMLU-Pro ~3.9pp at 120. Rankings separated by less than those margins are not established. - Temperature 0 throughout. Deterministic, reproducible, and not how the models will be used. Some models' instruction adherence degrades at temperature; this document would not see it. - One machine, one build lineage. Vulkan on gfx1151 with a specific llama.cpp build. Scores are hardware-robust in a way speeds are not, but kernel-specific numerical differences are unmeasured. - Caps differ between rows (512-8192). This is the largest single source of misleading comparisons in the tables, which is why §7.3 exists and why every score in this document carries its cap. - Family samples are tiny. n = 2-7. The Δ values in Table 5 are observed spreads, i.e. lower bounds; the true variance of a family is at least that and probably larger. - Selection effects. These models were not sampled randomly — they were chosen because they looked promising, which biases every family's apparent quality upward relative to the open population.

13.2 What the numbers mean in practice

The capacity wall is the real dividing line. A 128 GB Strix Halo runs a 120B model at Q4 and a 74 GiB model comfortably. A 24 GB RTX 4090 cannot run either; it tops out around 30B at Q4, and a 70B model requires two cards with tensor-parallel plumbing. If your goal is "run the biggest open models locally", unified memory is the cheap path and discrete VRAM is the expensive one.

Bandwidth is the price you pay for that capacity. Decode speed is roughly bandwidth divided by bytes read per token. This box reads 13.4 GiB per token for its driver at 59.5 t/s; a 4090 with roughly 4x the bandwidth would decode the same model 3-4x faster if the model fit, which for anything above ~30B it does not. The comparison only becomes fair when you compare models that fit on both machines, and at that point the Nvidia card is 3-4x faster per token and the Strix box runs models the card cannot hold at all.

Prefill is where Strix Halo is genuinely weak. No tensor cores means prompt processing runs at 350-2900 t/s versus several thousand to tens of thousands on modern Nvidia parts. For chat with short prompts this is invisible. For an agent pushing 30k-token contexts it is the difference between a 2-second and a 60-second wait. If your workload is long-prompt-heavy, this is the wrong machine and no amount of model choice fixes it.

Apple sits between them. M-series Max/Ultra have unified memory and high bandwidth (546-819 GB/s), with Metal and MLX being genuinely good, mature paths for local inference. An M4 Max with 128 GB will decode the same model roughly 2x faster than this box. What it does not have is CUDA, so the Nvidia-specific ecosystem is equally unavailable, and it does not have upgradeable RAM or a user-serviceable OS. It is the better appliance; the Strix box is the better laboratory.

Hosted frontier models as a reference point

Everything up to here answers "which model should this machine run". This section answers a different question, and it is the one a buyer asks first: how far behind is a local model on this box, and what does closing the gap cost?

Four hosted models were measured with the same harness, the same prompts, the same caps and the same scoring as the local roster. They are not roster members and do not appear in the league tables — the comparison is between systems, not weights, and mixing them into a per-axis ranking would invite exactly the cross-context ranking §8 forbids.

14.1 Method, and what is and is not equalised

The harness speaks HTTP to 127.0.0.1:<port> and reads llama.cpp's timings block. Hosted models are reached through ~/bench/api-shim.py, a ~150-line proxy that forwards to an OpenAI-compatible endpoint, streams the reply so time-to-first-token is measurable, and synthesises that block.

14.3 Five findings

1. Code is saturated. The local box is at statistical parity with the frontier. Sonnet and Haiku both score 0.9756 (160/164); the best local model scores 0.9634 (164 items). That is a 1.2pp gap against a standard error of ~1.3pp at n=164 — inside the noise. Put plainly: on HumanEval, at these caps, a 30B dense model on a desk-side box is not measurably worse than a hosted frontier model. Anyone buying this hardware primarily to write code should know that the marginal quality gain from the API is small.

2. Knowledge is where the frontier separates — and the cheap tier does not. Sonnet reaches 0.9250 MMLU-Pro against the best local score currently on disk, 0.8500 (gemma4-26b-uncen) — and 0.8667 for the retired gemma4-31b — a 5.8-7.5pp gap that is well outside the ~3.9pp standard error at n=120. But Haiku 5.5 scores 0.7583: 9.2pp below the current local driver (gemma4-26b-uncen, 0.8500) and below even the retired gemma4-31b (0.8667). "Frontier" is not a property of a vendor's family, it is a property of a model tier: the cheap hosted tier loses to a local 30B on factual knowledge while beating it on code. That single row is the best argument in this document for measuring rather than assuming.

3. Two routes for one model disagree — and not in the same direction. The pinned and unpinned DeepSeek V4.1 Flash routes are the same model family, and they split: the pinned snapshot is stronger on code (0.9512 vs 0.9207) while the unpinned route is stronger on knowledge (0.9167 vs 0.8917) at a fifth of the input price and with 97.7% prompt caching. Nothing in the weights explains that; the routes differ in serving configuration. It is the cleanest illustration in this document that a hosted row describes a deployment.

4. The hosted mid-tier is the value outlier. DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (pinned) scores 0.9512 on code — exactly the local 122B's score — and 0.8917 on knowledge, which is +10pp over the local 122B and +2.5pp over the best local model of any size, at $0.02/$1.00 per Mtok. It also produced 12/14 on the traps, matching the local 122B. If the workload is knowledge-heavy rather than latency-critical, this is the strongest quality-per-currency result measured anywhere in this document — including the local roster, whose marginal cost is electricity but whose quality ceiling is 0.8667.

5. Reasoning tokens are billed and invisible. The pinned DeepSeek route reported 24% of its output tokens as reasoning; Haiku 10%; Sonnet and the unpinned DeepSeek route 0%. Two consequences: the caps in this table are not comparable in effective budget between models, and a per-token price list understates the cost of a reasoning model by roughly the reasoning share.

The cap-burn counts make the same point from the other side: Haiku and Sonnet burned zero items at the 4096 cap on either axis, while the pinned DeepSeek route burned 4 of 164 on code and 7 of 120 on knowledge, and the unpinned route 11 and 6. So the DeepSeek rows are, if anything, slightly understated — their scores were earned with less room than the Claude rows had, and the same 4096 cap does not buy the same amount of answer from each route.

The route that caches is also the route that scored 0.9207 on code against the snapshot's 0.9512 — so cheap-per-call and strong-on-code do not coincide here. Read the two tables together: prefer the pinned snapshot when code quality decides, the unpinned route when prompt reuse dominates.

One further detail from the same probe, which matters for how caching is sold: on the route that caches, the 97.7% hit rate cut cost per call from $0.00063 to $0.00004 — a 94% saving — while time-to-first-token did not fall (1.45s cold against 1.84s averaged over the warm calls). So prompt caching here is a billing optimisation, not a latency one: the prefix is not re-billed, but the request still waits. Treat "caching enabled" as a cost claim until a latency measurement says otherwise.

Two notes that matter for budgeting. First, the route with the higher headline input price ($0.11 vs $0.02 per Mtok) is the one that caches, so a prompt-reuse workload costs less on it — the nominal price list ranks them the wrong way round. Second, the two Claude tiers report no automatic caching at all on this route: Anthropic's caching requires explicit cache breakpoints in the request, which a generic OpenAI-compatible shim does not emit. A harness that resends a long system prompt would therefore pay full price for every call, where a local model pays nothing because the prefix stays in its KV cache.

This is the mirror image of the local situation. Locally, prompt caching is free and automatic (cache_prompt), and its cost is RAM. On a hosted route it is a billing feature that may or may not be enabled, and it can move per-call cost by an order of magnitude.

14.5 Determinism and latency

Five identical calls at temperature 0 returned the identical answer on all four routes (the bat-and-ball trap, 0.05, every time). Hosted temperature-0 inference is reproducible in practice, which is worth stating because it is often assumed otherwise.

Latency, measured with a forced 400-token answer so that buffering cannot masquerade as fast streaming:

The honest reading: a hosted model's first token arrives in about the same time as the local driver's, because network latency and local prefill happen to be similar at short prompts. The hosted model then generates 4-13x faster. But the local driver's decode rate does not depend on how many other users exist, its cost does not scale with use, and at long context the hosted models' advantage in latency narrows sharply while their per-token cost keeps climbing.

14.6 Threats to validity specific to this comparison

  • Unknown quantisation and serving stack. A hosted score describes a system the reader cannot

inspect. The local rows can be reproduced exactly; the hosted rows cannot. - Provider drift. Pinned snapshots are stable; aliases are not. Every hosted row carries a date for this reason, and any re-read months later should be treated as a new measurement. - Effective budget differs by reasoning share (0-24% measured), so a fixed 4096 cap is not the same amount of answer budget across rows. - Cost is not comparable to local cost. Local marginal cost per sweep is electricity plus wear; hosted cost is per token and per cache behaviour. A like-for-like comparison would need this machine's power draw over the run, which was not instrumented here. - Selection. The four hosted models were chosen because they are the plausible reference points for this workload, not because they are the strongest available at any price. - n = 1 run per axis, as throughout. Differences under ~1.3pp (HE) and ~3.9pp (MMLU) are not established.

14.7 What would change the recommendation

The local roster is defensible today because code — the axis where the gap is measurably zero — is also the axis this box is used for most. Three things would move that:

  1. A knowledge-heavy workload. The +5.8pp (Sonnet) or +10pp (DeepSeek over the local 122B) gap

on MMLU-Pro is real and outside noise. If factual recall dominates, the API is worth its price.

  1. A privacy or availability requirement. Every hosted number here assumes the network and a

third-party account are acceptable. For a WhatsApp channel that must answer while travelling, the local model's failure mode is "slow"; a hosted model's failure mode is "offline". 3. A cheaper pinned snapshot with caching. The DeepSeek route demonstrates that quality near the frontier is available at $0.02/Mtok with 97% cache hits. If that combination persists, the economics of keeping a 26B model resident for knowledge work rather than code needs revisiting.

Uncensored and abliterated models: what the removal actually costs

Three different things get sold as "uncensored", and they behave differently enough that conflating them ruins the comparison:

   ABLITERATED    the refusal direction is found in activation space, then projected out of
                  the weights (orthogonalised). The model keeps its training and loses one
                  direction of behaviour. Small, surgical, and measurable.

   UNCENSORED     a finetune on unfiltered data, or trained with refusal pairs removed.
                  A different model with a different lineage — not the same weights minus
                  a direction.

   MERGE / NSFW   a roleplay or image-oriented merge, often of a different base entirely.
                  Unrelated to either of the above, despite the shared vocabulary.

Only the first kind is a controlled experiment. Everything below is about abliteration, and the document refuses to draw conclusions about the other two from it.

16.1 Why removal could cost capability

A refusal direction is not an isolated module. Projecting it out of the weights removes that direction from every weight matrix that happens to have a component along it, so the perturbation lands in whatever else the model encoded in that direction. In principle the casualties should be the behaviours that lean on the same structure: instruction-following precision, multi-step reasoning, and the consistency of long derivations — and code, which is largely pattern-and-syntax, should be the least affected. The measurements agree with that prediction, on the one pair available.

Abliteration is bought for one reason: the model stops refusing. On eight prompts chosen to sit near a safety boundary while having legitimate answers (SQL injection explained for defenders, bleach-ammonia chemistry, pin-tumbler mechanics, shoplifting fiction, ransomware concepts, drug-policy argument, gunpowder history, buffer overflows), run at a 1200-token cap:

   gpt-oss base          refused 0 of 8
   gpt-oss abliterated   refused 0 of 8

The base model of this family does not refuse this class of request in the first place, so the abliterated build had nothing to remove that these probes could see. Two conclusions, both practical:

  1. Abliteration is only worth its cost when the base model actually refuses your workload. Check

that first with a handful of real prompts. Buying an abliterated build to remove refusals the base model never made is paying a capability price for nothing. 2. "Uncensored" is not a quality tier. Both members of this pair answer the same questions, at the same speed, with the same code score. The difference lives in a narrow behavioural band, not in the axes this document measures.

16.4 The case that must not be attributed to censoring

The other abliterated artifact in the roster is qwen38fn-abl (176.9B, IQ3_S): HumanEval 0.8476, but MMLU-Pro 0.0083 — one correct answer in 120. That is not abliteration damage; it is a 3.4-bit quantisation of a 177B model, and §5.6 already documents the same collapse in a non-abliterated file. There is no matched censored comparator at the same quant, so the row says nothing about abliteration either way.

The same trap catches the boot model. Ornith-1.5-35B-Uncensored-YMQ-M (15.1 GiB, IQ3_XXS) was measured on 2026-10-31 at cap 4096 — 0.5671 code, 0.6333 knowledge, 12/14 riddles — and at 0.57 it looks like a 26.8-point ablation bill against the 0.8354 that the standard Ornith-1.5-35B build scores at the same cap since 2026-11-01. What the run does establish is that the cap is not the explanation — re-measuring at four times the budget moved one code item — so the honest label is "an IQ3-class uncensored fine-tune, not a clean abliteration datapoint".

And one file that looks unmeasured is not. 17.1 What changed in the model, in plain terms

Four architectural choices matter for how fast and how cheaply these models run:

  • Sparse attention with an indexer. Instead of every new token attending to every earlier token,

a small "indexer" scores the past and only the top few are attended. Cost per token grows much more slowly with context than in a conventional transformer. (llama.cpp's own deepseek4.cpp implements this with an indexer and top-k selection, and ships llama-kv-cache-dsa.cpp — "DeepSeek Sparse Attention" — so the mechanism is inspectable on this machine.) - Compressed key/value state. Queries and keys pass through low-rank projections (q_lora in the same source), so the KV cache per token is far smaller than a same-width dense model's. This is why million-token contexts are economic at all. - Mixture of experts with few active experts. The model holds many parameters and reads only a small fraction per token (n_expert). Total size sets memory; active size sets speed. - Multi-token prediction heads (nextn/mtp). The model proposes several future tokens, which a serving engine can use as drafts for speculative decoding.

The through-line: these changes reduce both the bytes read and the attention work per generated token. That is exactly the quantity that governs performance on a bandwidth-limited machine like the one this document is about — and exactly what a serving engine can then exploit.

17.2 What changed in the engine

DeepSeek open-sourced DeepInfer on 2026-10-20, described as the framework behind their production service, supporting precisely DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash. The features that explain the measurements here:

   kernel suite           FlashMLA, DeepGEMM, DeepEP, DeepSelect, TileKernels — fused attention,
                          matrix-multiply and token-dispatch kernels instead of generic ones
   expert parallelism     experts spread across GPUs, with EPLB rebalancing the hot ones
     + EPLB
   DSpark / NEXTN         speculative decoding using the model's own multi-token-prediction heads;
                          default maximum draft length 5
   SWA bounded replay     do not re-read the whole prompt: replay only a bounded suffix (the V4.1
                          default keeps ~140 tokens) when the model's sliding-window attention
                          makes the rest irrelevant
   async scheduling       overlap the CPU's batch bookkeeping with GPU execution
   deterministic          batch-invariant inference + deterministic sampling: same tokens in,
     sampling             same tokens out, regardless of what else is in the batch
   structured outputs     constrain generation to a regex / JSON schema / grammar

   hardware: NVIDIA SM100 and Huawei Ascend 950 only. Not consumer GPUs, not Vulkan, not this box.
   published benchmarks: 4 SM100 GPUs for prefill, 64 for decode.

Note what is not in the open release: a KV-cache system, prefill/decode disaggregation, and cross-replica load balancing. It is a serving stack for a fleet, not a single-machine runtime.

17.4 What this means for the comparison

The speed gap is a systems gap, not a weights gap. The local box and the hosted fleet are not running the same kind of thing. Locally you get one model, one GPU, one sequence, no expert parallelism, no paged KV at fleet scale, no draft tokens in the build used here, and no bounded SWA replay. The hosted fleet gets fused kernels, dozens of GPUs, speculation and caching. When the same nominal model served on two hosted routes differed by 2.9x in decode rate, that is the clearest possible demonstration that these numbers describe deployments.

The quality gap is a weights gap, and it is the durable one. HumanEval and MMLU-Pro scores are properties of the model. They will still be true next month; the t/s figures will not.

This box could, in principle, run the architecture — it cannot run the weights. llama.cpp implements DeepSeek V4 (deepseek4.cpp, the DSA KV caches, the indexer), so the software is not the obstacle. The obstacle is arithmetic: the smallest published quantisation of DeepSeek V4.1 Flash is 120.5 GiB (measured during the candidate scans, not re-derived here) against a 100 GiB pool. Even with full kernel support there is nowhere to put it.

17.5 The practical takeaway

For a buyer or a newcomer, three sentences:

  1. Do not read a hosted tokens-per-second figure as "this model is fast". Read it as "this

deployment is fast", and expect the same weights to be 3-10x slower anywhere else. 2. Do read a hosted accuracy figure as the model's, because that part travels — subject to the caps and reasoning-budget caveats in §7.3 and §14.6. 3. Architecture improvements make local serving cheaper faster than they make it faster. Sparse attention and compressed KV cut memory per token, which is why a million-token context is now ordinary; they do not remove the bandwidth ceiling that sets local decode speed.

18.2 Pitfalls specific to this platform

  1. Old Mesa is the number one cause of nonsense output. Below Mesa 25.x on RDNA 3.5 you get

wrong tokens, not errors. Check glxinfo -B before blaming the model. 2. The first run of a new model is not a benchmark. Vulkan shader compilation makes the first numbers 2-5x too low. Warm up once. 3. GTT is not VRAM. mem_info_vram_total reading 8 GiB is expected; the usable pool is mem_info_gtt_total (100 GiB). Do not conclude the model will not fit from the VRAM number. 4. Context is a memory purchase. A 4B model at 1M tokens needed 20 GiB of KV. Decide context length before, not after, you run out of pool. 5. Quantisation is not free at the extremes. 2-bit preserves code and destroys knowledge (0.9451 → 0.3667 MMLU in one measured case). 3-bit is usually the practical floor. 6. Reasoning models need budget or they fail silently. A thinking model with a small cap returns an empty string, which scores zero. Either raise the cap or disable thinking. 7. Chat templates break more models than weights do. Two models here failed all probes for template reasons, not capability reasons. --jinja and a correct template matter. 8. Do not run a benchmark alongside anything else. Shared bandwidth means contention halves decode speed; a "slow model" may just have been measured during another run. 9. One session per camera, one model per GPU. Resource exclusivity is a design constraint here, not an inconvenience — it is why Conflicts= and the availability guard exist.

  1. Do not trust a repo name. A *-GGUF repo can contain diffusion weights (one measured

case declared arch = wan). Read the header before a 15 GiB download: head -c 4M model.gguf and parse the metadata.

23.1 Where the datasets overlap, and where they disagree

Exactly one model is measured on this GPU by both this report and pepuscz/strix-halo-llm: Qwen3.8-Flash-Next. Theirs, at Q4_XL on Ubuntu's Vulkan stack: 465 t/s prefill, 42.8 t/s decode at a 1.9k prompt. Ours, qwen38fn-abl at IQ3_S on CachyOS/RADV: 448 t/s prefill, 52.9 t/s decode. Different quant (smaller here, hence faster decode), different kernel and driver — the two are consistent rather than contradictory, which is the useful outcome: two independent stacks landing within 10 % of each other on prefill is evidence the harness is measuring the machine and not itself.

Their quality suite and this one are not comparable either, and the arithmetic says why: 30 items means one task is 3.3 pp, so "30/30" versus "29/30" is a single question. That is not a criticism of their suite — it is the right size for "did this deployment break the model" — but it cannot rank models, which is what 164 HumanEval items with executed tests and 120 MMLU-Pro items can do.

What this report borrowed after reading theirs: the prefill/decode split recorded per point rather than per run (§23.2), and the practice of hashing the prompt. What it did not borrow: their hardware assumptions, which are Ubuntu's ROCm/Vulkan stack on a different kernel — every throughput figure here would need re-measuring to be quoted against theirs.

Five depths, one run each, no retries needed. Retrieval held at every depth, including a 112,862-token prompt — the answer came back correct with the needle planted in the middle of the haystack.

What degrades is throughput, and the two curves are not the same shape. Prefill falls 2.6× from 1,715 t/s at 1.8k to 670 t/s at 113k, because attention cost grows with context. Decode falls 1.5×, from 57.2 to 37.7 t/s. For chat the decode number is the one that matters: a conversation that reaches 113k tokens answers about a third slower than one at 2k, and the cliff between 58k and 113k is steeper than the one before it. The practical reading is to compact well before 64k rather than at the edge of the window — the model is not forgetting anything, it is just getting slow.

This is the behaviour of the model that answers this machine's traffic, not of the best model on this machine — §9.1 explains why those differ.

23.3 Cross-validation on their suite — and what their harness had to fix

The strongest check available was to run their 30-item suite on our deployed driver, unchanged: same prompts, same per-item caps (512 / 1024 / 2048), temperature 0, coding answers executed against the fixture's own gold_test. Fixtures pinned at Luce-Org/lucebox@90f85fa, which is the commit their results cite.

Result: 30/30 — coding 10/10, GSM8K 10/10, math 10/10. The same score their Qwen3.8-Flash-Next deployment reported, from a different model on different hardware. Two independently built harnesses, same verdict: the suite is not measuring itself.

But the first pass scored 26/30, and every one of the four misses was the harness, not the model. That is worth recording, because it is the failure mode this report keeps warning about in its own axes:

itemfirst-pass verdictwhat actually happened
gsm_06FAIL, "got 16"answered $64 correctly; the grader took the last number in the sentence — "for the 16 glasses" — as the result
gsm_08FAIL, "got 40"answered 160 minutes correctly; same bug, via "(or 2 hours and 40 minutes)"
math_04FAIL, "got -2/3"answered −2/3, which is the gold -\frac{2}{3}; the grader compared LaTeX to plain notation without normalising
math_08FAIL, "got \frac{20"answered 20/3, which is correct; the fixture's gold_answer says 20 and the source's own results.json records "math_08 reference corrected to 20/3". A naive \boxed{…} regex also truncated the nested braces

Three grader defects and one fixture error, all on answers the model got right. Fixing them — answer-marker parsing, LaTeX-to-fraction normalisation, balanced-brace \boxed{} extraction, and the documented math_08 correction — gives the 30/30 above, and the run is auditable: ~/runs/lucebox30-driver-final.jsonl records, per item, the numbers found in the answer sentence and the raw final text.

The lesson generalises, and it cuts at this report too: a score is only as good as the grader, and the grader is usually not published. Their suite publishes its fixtures and hashes, which is why the four errors could be diagnosed rather than argued about. Any quality number quoted without its harness and its normalisation rules carries an unknown error bar — on 30 items, 4 items is 13 pp.
