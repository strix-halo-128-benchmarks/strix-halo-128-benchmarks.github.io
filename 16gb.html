A 16 GiB graphics card is the tightest interesting class of consumer accelerator: 16 GiB of VRAM, about 15 GiB usable once the driver and runtime have taken their share, and a hard wall at the end of it. This page answers two questions about that class. Which open-weight models fit, and what do they score. Then: how much of that answer depends on the memory architecture underneath, because the same 13.4 GiB file behaves very differently on a unified-memory APU, a discrete card and an Apple SoC.

Every number here was measured on a machine that does not have the constraint being studied. The reference host is a Minisforum MS-S1 MAX with no VRAM ceiling — a 100 GiB GTT window means nothing is truncated or spilled while it is being benchmarked. That is deliberate: the scores describe the model, the arithmetic describes the card, and the two are never confounded. Where a figure is arithmetic rather than measurement, it says so.

Cap, budget, and what the budget is spent on

Every number on this page is qualified by one of these, so they are worth fixing before the scores.

  • cap — the maximum number of tokens the model was allowed to generate for a single answer (max_tokens in a chat request, max_gen_toks in lm-eval). It is part of the measurement, not a setting: the same model at cap 512 and at 8192 is two different results, and a deliberating model at a small cap produces no answer at all — the reasoning block eats the budget and the failure reads as a wrong answer rather than a truncated one. Wherever a cap is unusual it is printed in the row it belongs to, because a score without its cap cannot be compared with anything.
  • budget — the memory a card can actually spend: 15 GiB on a 16 GiB card, after the driver and runtime have taken their share. Weights, the KV cache for the context, and about 1 GiB of runtime overhead all have to fit inside it. A model over budget does not run slowly, it does not run — so fit is settled before speed is discussed, and every table marks it: ok = at least 1 GiB of headroom left, tight = fits with less, NO = over budget. Three sentences on this page use the word fits and they are not the same test: fits with headroom (the ok flag in Table 2), fits at the context it was measured at (§1's context column), and fits at all — the arithmetic behind Table 7 and Figure 2. That is why Table 7 can count a model as fitting at 8k while §1 runs it at 4k.
  • footprint — how much of that budget a model actually takes: weights, plus KV at the context it is run at, plus the 1 GiB overhead. It is why a 6.26 GiB file can occupy 7.67 GiB, and why the same file at 32k costs more than at 8k.
  • KV cache — per-conversation memory for attention. It grows linearly with context and is set by the model's attention layout, not its size: on this roster a 14B dense model costs 160 MiB per 1k tokens against a 26B MoE's 20 — attention layout, not size, sets the bill (§6).

None of these is a preference. Cap is a fact about the run, budget is arithmetic about the card, footprint and KV are what the arithmetic is spent on.

Each axis can also be satisfied without doing the thing it is named after — which is the part that makes the roster disagree with itself:

  • HumanEval rewards code shape. 0.90 here and the model may still be unable to say why the function works.
  • MMLU-Pro rewards discrimination between near-neighbours. Know the topic, miss the distinction, and the score is indistinguishable from knowing neither.
  • IFEval rewards compliance. Format it correctly and the marks are yours even when the content is wrong.

That is why the winners differ by axis (§3), and why the mean of the three is only a way to order a shortlist — read the column that matches your workload.

4. How to read the numbers

  • Quality does not depend on the GPU. HumanEval, MMLU-Pro, IFEval, the needle probes and the protocol checks are hardware-independent and transfer to a card unchanged (Table 9).
  • Decode ranks transfer, absolute rates do not. Decode is bandwidth-bound on bytes read per token on all three architectures this page compares — the unified APU, a discrete card, an Apple SoC — so the ordering survives the hop even though the rates do not: the MS-S1 MAX sits at ~256 GB/s against 288-896 GB/s on a 16 GB card and 546-819 GB/s on Apple silicon.
  • Prefill does not transfer at all. It is compute-bound, and an MoE's prefill touches every expert that receives a token, so a discrete card pulls further ahead on exactly the rows where the numbers below look slowest.
  • Caps and sample sizes are part of the result. One standard error is ±2.3 points on HumanEval (164 items at a score of 0.90), ±4.5 on MMLU-Pro (120 at 0.61) and ±3.8 on IFEval (50 prompts at 0.92). Twice those — roughly eight points on IFEval, nine on MMLU-Pro — is the smallest difference worth arguing about. The whiskers on Figures 1 and 4 are 95% confidence intervals — the range the score would fall in on a fresh sample of the same size, computed as ±1.96 × √(p(1-p)/n) over the item count, which is about double the standard errors quoted here. That is what the "95% CI" legend on Figure 1 and the panel title on Figure 4 mean: the text quotes one standard error, the charts draw 95% intervals, and both say which they are so a number cannot be read in the wrong units. MMLU-Pro here is a 0-shot chat probe, not the 5-shot loglikelihood protocol the public leaderboards use: the column is internally consistent and sits below published scores by design.

Two roster rows are deliberately absent: lfm25-24b-a2b is the thinking arm's row, because the KV belongs to the weights and not to the reasoning flag, and the MTP arm is left out because its draft head carries a KV cache of its own that is not a roster rate — §10 counts it. The 35B hybrid row repeats the 19B's numbers because both are qwen3.5moe hybrids with 10 full-attention blocks against 30 linear ones, and that layout is what makes 64k affordable at this footprint.

The practical rule: when a model does not fit, cut context first, then KV precision, then quantisation depth, and only then offload. Context is the cheapest thing to give up and the only one that costs no quality at all.

7. Dense or MoE

A dense model runs every parameter for every token. An MoE routes each token to a subset of experts, so a 26B model with 8 of 128 active does roughly the arithmetic of a 4B model while carrying 26B of weights. Footprint follows total parameters; decode speed follows active parameters; the two stop being proportional.

10. When it does not fit

The MTP row is the useful failure: 15.06 GiB at 8k context, over budget by 0.06 GiB. That is what the wall looks like — not a crash, but a model that loads and then spends its life at the edge of the pool. In order of cost:

  1. Cut context. Halving 32k to 16k returns 0.1-1.25 GiB depending on the model's attention layout (Table 5), and costs nothing in quality for prompts that fit.
  2. Quantise the KV cache. q8_0 is already assumed throughout; q4_0 halves it again at a small quality cost and is the standard second lever.
  3. Drop the drafter. A speculative-decoding head costs weights, and in general a second KV cache; on this roster the head is 0.43 GiB, and that is the whole difference between g4-26b-a4b-qat at 14.63 GiB and the MTP row's 15.06 — over the wall by 0.06. Dropping it recovers exactly that, and costs no quality: the weights are unchanged.
  4. Drop quantisation depth. One step down is roughly 0.5-1 GiB on a 25B model. It is the only lever that changes what the model knows.
  5. Offload layers. -ngl below the layer count moves layers to CPU, which costs PCIe or system-memory bandwidth on every token — the 5-10x decode collapse that a discrete card shows on spill.

Every row above ran with the same flags, listed once in Table 1's server-flags row: -ngl 99 (all layers on the GPU), -c at the context measured rather than the model's maximum, --flash-attn on, --cache-type-k q8_0 --cache-type-v q8_0, -np 1 (one slot — concurrency multiplies KV), -b 4096 -ub 2048 for prefill batching. None of them is exotic; the discipline is that each one changes what fits, which is the list above.

11. Three memory architectures

Figure 7 — Peak memory bandwidth on a log scale, with the PCIe spill path for comparison. Bandwidth sets decode; whether there is a hard ceiling at all decides what runs. Bandwidth is quoted in decimal GB/s, as vendors publish it, while capacities on this page are GiB: 256 GB/s is 238 GiB/s.

  • Unified APU (the MS-S1 MAX). 128 GiB of LPDDR5X-8000 on a 256-bit bus, ~256 GB/s, with a 100 GiB GTT window on top of an 8 GiB carve-out. No wall to cross, so a 40 GiB model runs — at the speed 256 GB/s allows.
  • Discrete card. 24 GB at 1008 GB/s or 32 GB at 1792 GB/s: four to seven times the bandwidth on a fifth of the capacity. Over the VRAM limit, layers spill to host memory across PCIe (~64 GB/s on a 5.0 x16 link) and decode falls by roughly an order of magnitude. In this class the card is not capacity-constrained at all — the whole roster fits at 24 GB.
  • Apple Silicon. Unified like the APU, with a wider bus: 546 GB/s on an M4 Max, 819 GB/s on an M3 Ultra, capacities from 36 GB to 512 GB. No hard wall, a driver-capped GPU share, and a Metal rather than CUDA/ROCm software path.

How the prediction is made, so it can be checked and discounted: decode is bandwidth-bound, so predicted = bandwidth / bytes-read-per-active-token × efficiency, where efficiency is the fraction of the ceiling the hardware actually reaches. The MS-S1 MAX measures 53% of its own ceiling on the MoE (59 of 111 tok/s) and 73% on the dense model (28 of 38) — and 43% on axion-1.0-35b-iq3, which reads 1.18 GB per active token at 3.14 bpw (94 of 217). Both measured values sit inside their bands, which is the only calibration available without borrowing a card.

Three things follow, and one warning:

  • A 128-bit 16 GB card buys capacity at the cost of speed. The four 128-bit boards — RTX 4060 Ti and RX 7600 XT at 288 GB/s, RX 9060 XT at 320, RTX 5060 Ti at 448 — are the cheapest ways to hold 16 GB, and they decode at 0.75-1.85x the MS-S1 MAX. If the plan is "run a 13 GiB MoE", they hold it and they run it slowly.
  • The 256-bit cards are where the value is for this workload. 512-672 GB/s covers the RX 6800, the Arc A770, the RX 6950 XT / 7900 GRE / 7800 XT / 9070 and the 4070 Ti Super: 1.3-2.7x the MS-S1 MAX's decode for the same 16 GB, which turns the 26B MoE from usable into comfortable. AMD is the wider choice here — five boards between 512 and 640 GB/s, with NVIDIA's nearest the 4070 Ti Super at 672.
  • The 5070 Ti is the top of the class and pays for it. 896 GB/s is 3.5x the MS-S1 MAX, and it is the only 16 GB card here whose dense-model band sits fully above 70 tok/s.
  • Warning: bandwidth does not buy prefill in the same proportion. These predictions cover decode only. Prefill is compute-bound and an MoE's prefill touches every expert, so every card in this table will beat its decode-side prediction on long prompts by a margin the MS-S1 MAX cannot show — the same caveat that applies to Table 9. On the AMD side the software path matters too: these cards run through ROCm or Vulkan on Linux, and backend efficiency, not the memory spec, is what moves prefill.

13. What this page does not measure

Listed because the gaps are the useful part of a benchmark:

  • Agentic tool use. Preflight confirms an OpenAI-shaped client can read the reply (3/3 on every row: non-empty content, a JSON object, a one-word answer). A multi-round tool loop was not run, and a model can pass preflight and still fail to drive tools.
  • Long-context retrieval per model. The roster's needle probes are at 2k and 8k, and the two 4k-context rows were not given the 8k probe at all. A depth curve to 100k+ was run for other models on this platform, not for these.
  • Energy and thermals per token. The envelope is measured and quoted with the platform table — 96 W mean package power against a 129 W peak, 69 °C mean GPU temperature against an 88 °C hottest sample — but it is a thermal record sampled every ~2 s, not an energy-per-1k-tokens figure, which would need the integral over a run.
  • Concurrency. Every row was measured with one slot. Throughput under -np 4 is a different measurement with a different KV footprint.
  • Quant ladders per model. One quant per model was measured. The claim that QAT holds up better than post-training quant at equal bpw rests on the controlled pair, not on a sweep.
  • Non-autoregressive decoding. clover-35b-a3b-iq2 is measured, but only in the mode llama.cpp can run: causal decode, one token at a time. The model was trained for parallel block diffusion, and nothing here measures that — so the arithmetic that makes the architecture interesting (a whole block decoded at once) is outside this page's evidence entirely. The row is an accurate measurement of the causal arm and no statement at all about the parallel one. It is worth naming as a gap even though the row exists, because the row's numbers are good enough that they will be quoted without the caveat.
  • Real spill behaviour. This host cannot show it. The spill penalty quoted for discrete cards is from the literature and the PCIe arithmetic, not from a measurement on a card.
